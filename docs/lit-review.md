# Reuse a published Qwen3-8B EAGLE-3 head

Adding a draft head to a model that was not trained with one is now routine engineering, and for Qwen3-8B it has already been done at least five times, so the reader most likely does not need to train anything. The practical standard is EAGLE-3: one transformer decoder layer of about 0.4B parameters that reads the frozen trunk's hidden states and is trained on roughly 0.5M regenerated conversations (about 1B tokens), at a cost that one paper puts at 200-300 GPU-hours. **Four independent EAGLE-3 heads for Qwen3-8B are public (RedHatAI, AngelSlim, Tengyunw, WhiFlash), and all of them build on the base model's own hidden states rather than being separate draft models.** Their model cards cannot tell you which is the best predictor of Qwen3-8B's future tokens, because each was measured with a different server, draft shape, temperature and benchmark; a matched evaluation of the four is being run separately and its results are not in this report. Co-training the trunk matters less than expected for heads that are given the previously drafted token: it buys roughly 80-90% first-token acceptance in native modules, against about 75-80% for frozen-trunk retrofits, and every documented improvement at deeper draft positions was obtained with the trunk frozen. Three caveats matter for interpretability use: EAGLE-3 heads read three trunk layers rather than only the last, they predict over a reduced 32K vocabulary, and the Qwen3-8B heads that state their setting were trained with thinking mode off. A last-layer-only MTP block in the DeepSeek/Qwen3.5 style has been retrofitted to Qwen3-8B by Tencent's AngelSpec, but no released checkpoint was confirmed, so that architecture still means training your own.

## Feeding the head the previous draft token lifts acceptance from 60% to 75-80%

The retrofit literature since 2024 converges on one design lesson. Medusa attaches parallel heads to the last hidden state, each guessing a further-ahead position without seeing the tokens in between; with the trunk frozen it reaches 2.18x on Vicuna-7B after only 5 A100-hours of training on 60k ShareGPT samples ([Medusa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)). An independent measurement puts its per-token acceptance at **60.5% versus 74.6% for EAGLE-1** on the same model ([Falcon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.12639)). Every later family closes that gap the same way, by conditioning each draft step on the embedding of the previously drafted token: Hydra and Hydra++ ([Hydra](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05109)), Clover-2 ([Clover-2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00264)), Falcon, EAGLE, and the DeepSeek/Qwen MTP block itself. Hydra++ lands at 1.01x of EAGLE-1's throughput and 0.92x of its acceptance length; Clover-2 and Amphista report parity or small wins over EAGLE-1 in their own harnesses ([Amphista](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13170)). So EAGLE is not the only approach that works, but none of these alternatives has a Qwen3-8B checkpoint or a maintained toolchain, and none has been compared against EAGLE-3.

On the one widely used neutral benchmark, the ordering is EAGLE family, then Hydra, then Medusa and the training-free methods. Spec-Bench on an A100 with Vicuna-7B gives EAGLE-2 2.36x (4.34 accepted tokens), EAGLE 2.10x, Hydra 2.07x, Token Recycling 2.22x and Medusa 1.71x; its only EAGLE-3 entry is Vicuna-13B at **3.02x with 5.71 accepted tokens** ([Spec-Bench leaderboard](https://github.com/hemingkx/Spec-Bench/blob/main/Leaderboard.md)). Training-free methods mostly sit at 1.1-1.7x on chat workloads. On Qwen3-8B specifically, the training-free ESP method reaches only 1.37-1.43x with acceptance length 1.63-1.74, and prompt lookup 1.12x ([ESP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17942)). Self-speculative layer skipping gives 1.2-2.0x ([Draft & Verify](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08168)). These methods are useful baselines but they are not learned predictors of future tokens, so they do not serve the reader's purpose.

Natively trained MTP modules are the other end of the spectrum. DeepSeek-V3 co-trains one extra transformer block that fuses the trunk's hidden state with the next token's embedding, with loss weight 0.3 then 0.1, and reports 85-90% acceptance for the second token and 1.8x throughput ([DeepSeek-V3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437)). Qwen3-Next and Qwen3.5 ship the same kind of block; the only official description for Qwen3.5 is "MTP: trained with multi-steps" ([Qwen3.5-9B model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B)). Community measurements of the Qwen3.5-4B native head show 88.8% / 82.6% / 72.5% acceptance at draft depths 1 / 2 / 3 ([graft-mtp](https://github.com/MH221B/graft-mtp)). On module size: the roughly 120.6M-parameter figure belongs to **Qwen3.5-4B** (hidden size 2560) and was measured directly from that model's safetensors headers, so it is correct for that size. The larger Qwen3.5-9B block (hidden size 4096) works out to roughly 227M parameters from its config, which is an estimate and not a published count, and it agrees with the 210.8M stated for MiMo-7B's block at the same hidden size ([FastMTP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18362)).

## EAGLE-3 is the standard recipe: one layer, about 1B tokens, a few hundred GPU-hours

EAGLE has three versions that share one head design. EAGLE-1 trains a single decoder layer on the frozen model's top-layer features with a feature-regression loss plus 0.1 times a token loss, on 68k ShareGPT dialogues, in 1-2 days on RTX 3090-class hardware ([EAGLE](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.15077)). EAGLE-2 retrains nothing and only changes the draft tree at inference ([EAGLE-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16858)). EAGLE-3 makes three changes that matter here: it drops the feature loss and predicts tokens directly, it feeds the head a fusion of low-, mid- and high-layer trunk features, and it unrolls the head on its own outputs during training ("training-time test"). On Llama-3.1-8B these changes raise acceptance length from 4.05 (EAGLE-2) to 6.13 on MT-bench, with the fused multi-layer input alone accounting for the step from 5.37 to 6.13 ([EAGLE-3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840)). The follow-ups HASS, FSPAD and CORAL each add about 0.3-0.6 accepted tokens over EAGLE-2 at 1.7-3x the training compute ([HASS](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.15766), [CORAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16880v1)); EAGLE-3 absorbs their central idea of multi-step training, so none of them is the better starting point today.

The recipe is good, but the headline numbers are best-case. EAGLE-3's own paper reports 4.40x for Llama-3.1-8B in the authors' harness and **2.36x for the same model and benchmark inside SGLang on an H100** ([EAGLE-3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840)). Acceptance length of 5-6 requires greedy decoding and a 60-token draft tree; with a simple chain of draft tokens it saturates near 2.3-2.8. For a reader who cares about prediction quality and not throughput, the lesson is that acceptance length is a property of the head plus the draft shape plus the sampling settings, never of the head alone.

A standard recipe does exist, and three maintained toolkits ship it with a ready Qwen3-8B configuration: speculators from the vLLM project ([speculators tutorial](https://docs.vllm.ai/projects/speculators/en/latest/user_guide/tutorials/train/)), SpecForge from the SGLang team ([SpecForge](https://github.com/sgl-project/SpecForge)), and Tencent's AngelSpec ([AngelSlim](https://github.com/Tencent/AngelSlim)). The official EAGLE repository now recommends SpecForge for EAGLE-3 training ([EAGLE README](https://github.com/SafeAILab/EAGLE)). The shared ingredients are a frozen trunk, one decoder layer, a 32K draft vocabulary, an unroll length of 7, and training responses regenerated by the target model itself, which SpecForge's team calls "the largest lever on final acceptance" ([LMSYS blog](https://lmsys.org/blog/2026-08-04-specforge-v0-3)).

Data requirements are well bracketed. The EAGLE-3 paper uses ShareGPT (about 68K entries) plus UltraChat-200K (about 464K entries), roughly 8x the EAGLE-1 data, and is the first version whose quality keeps rising with data ([EAGLE-3](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840)). One Qwen3-8B head reports "about 600K samples and 1B tokens" ([Tengyunw card](https://huggingface.co/Tengyunw/qwen3_8b_eagle3)), and SpecForge's production heads use 1.4M conversations for 2 epochs ([SpecForge paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18567)). Too little data fails visibly: 50,000 ShareGPT conversations for 5 epochs gave acceptance length 1.9 on a Qwen2.5-7B head ([EAGLE issue #276](https://github.com/SafeAILab/EAGLE/issues/276)), and 5K-sample sanity runs reach about 1.5 ([speculators tutorial](https://docs.vllm.ai/projects/speculators/en/latest/user_guide/tutorials/train/)).

Compute is the least well documented part. **No source publishes an end-to-end GPU-hour figure for an EAGLE-3 head on a 7-8B model.** The EAGLE-3 paper states no hardware or wall-clock; the best-documented Qwen3-8B head used 4x H100 for 800K steps at batch size 8 without stating hours ([WhiFlash card](https://huggingface.co/WhiFlash/Qwen3-8B-EAGLE3)); the ESP paper puts EAGLE-3 training at "200-300 GPU hours" ([ESP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17942)). SpecForge measured 126,640 training tokens per second on 8x H200 for Llama-3.1-8B, which has the same hidden size as Qwen3-8B ([SpecForge paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18567)). From that throughput, the notes estimate about 17.5 H200-hours per billion token-epochs, so a 2-epoch run over 1B tokens costs tens of GPU-hours and a paper-faithful 14-epoch run costs a few hundred. That estimate is arithmetic on cited inputs and is not a reported figure. Hidden states should be generated online: caching them offline costs about 268 MB per 8192-token sample in the speculators example, or 13 TB for 50K samples ([speculators tutorial](https://docs.vllm.ai/projects/speculators/en/latest/user_guide/tutorials/train/)).

## Co-training buys first-token accuracy; deeper positions are fixed with the trunk frozen

No paper trains the same MTP block both jointly from pretraining and post hoc on a frozen checkpoint of the same model, so the central question has no clean answer. The indirect evidence points in a consistent direction.

A frozen trunk does cost something for heads that cannot see the intermediate token. Medusa goes from 2.18x frozen to 2.83x with joint LoRA training on Vicuna-7B ([Medusa](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)). Mehra et al. find second-token top-5 accuracy of 0.50-0.61 with a frozen backbone against 0.59-0.67 for their best joint variant, and conclude that the strongest MTP models "likely require full MTP pretraining" ([Mehra et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09419)). Both studies use parallel heads. The penalty shrinks for sequential heads, because giving the head token t+1 turns the task into ordinary next-token prediction from a slightly stale state, which is why EAGLE, Hydra and Falcon reach 75-80% acceptance on frozen trunks.

Joint training of an already-pretrained model is also risky. Gloeckle et al. report that fine-tuning Llama 2 with an MTP loss "changes the initialization too brutally and never really recovers" ([Gloeckle et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)). GrowMTP's joint cross-entropy arm on Qwen3.5-4B reached the highest acceptance length (3.99) while its held-out task quality fell to zero; training the head on detached backbone states gave 3.18-3.22 with quality preserved ([GrowMTP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16648)). For interpretability this settles the choice: a head trained on a frozen trunk leaves the model under study unchanged, and a co-trained one does not.

Native co-training mainly helps the first drafted token. Native heads used recursively without extra work collapse quickly: MiMo-7B's head accepts about 70% at step 1, 10% at step 2 and almost nothing at step 3. Fine-tuning only that head with everything else frozen, in under a day on one H20 server, lifts the three steps to 81% / 56% / 36% ([FastMTP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18362)). Tencent's Hy3 shows the same pattern: frozen-backbone recursive training moves cumulative acceptance from 0.799 / 0.518 / 0.266 to 0.814 / 0.653 / 0.524, a gain of 1.5 points at position 1 and 25.8 points at position 3 ([AngelSpec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25852)). NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 "healing" and "boosting" stages likewise freeze the backbone and train only the head ([Nemotron 3 Ultra](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15007)). The working conclusion is that the retrofit handicap is real but modest for sequential heads, and that multi-step quality comes from recursive head-only training, which a retrofit can do as well as a native model can.

## Four Qwen3-8B heads are public, and their cards cannot rank them

Qwen3-8B is one of the best-covered bases for EAGLE-3. There are no official EAGLE-3 weights for Qwen3 from the EAGLE authors; their README lists community checkpoints and marks official Qwen3 support as an open to-do ([EAGLE README](https://github.com/SafeAILab/EAGLE)). The four heads of interest are all EAGLE-3 heads that consume the trunk's hidden states, which satisfies the reader's requirement.

| Head | Stated training data | Stated training setup | Card's evaluation setting | Card's headline number |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [RedHatAI/Qwen3-8B-speculator.eagle3](https://huggingface.co/RedHatAI/Qwen3-8B-speculator.eagle3) | ShareGPT + UltraChat-200k; amount not stated; thinking disabled | speculators library, from preliminary code adapted from EAGLE and HASS | vLLM 0.11.0, A100, chain of k tokens, T=0.6, top_p 0.95, top_k 20 | Acceptance length 2.13-2.48 at k=3, 2.30-2.81 at k=7 |
| [AngelSlim/Qwen3-8B_eagle3](https://huggingface.co/AngelSlim/Qwen3-8B_eagle3) | Not stated | Not stated | vLLM 0.11.2, H20, chain of 2, batch 1 | Acceptance length 1.99, about 1.70x |
| [Tengyunw/qwen3_8b_eagle3](https://huggingface.co/Tengyunw/qwen3_8b_eagle3) | UltraChat-200k prompts, responses regenerated by Qwen3-8B; about 600K samples, 1B tokens | Not stated | SGLang, 6 steps, top-k 10, 32 draft tokens | 187 to 365 tok/s on H200; no acceptance length |
| [WhiFlash/Qwen3-8B-EAGLE3](https://huggingface.co/WhiFlash/Qwen3-8B-EAGLE3) | About 464K UltraChat examples, responses regenerated by Qwen3-8B | 800K steps, batch 8, lr 5e-5, unroll length 7, 4x H100 | SGLang, H100, 8 steps, top-k 10, 60-token tree, Spec-Bench | Acceptance length 4.78, 2.42x |

**These numbers do not rank the heads on prediction quality.** WhiFlash's 4.78 comes from a 60-token draft tree, where many candidate continuations are verified at once. RedHatAI's 2.1-2.8 comes from a single chain under sampling at temperature 0.6. AngelSlim's 1.99 is capped by construction, because a chain of 2 draft tokens cannot exceed an acceptance length of 3. Tengyunw reports throughput only. The benchmarks, hardware and server versions also differ. The EAGLE README itself warns that unofficial checkpoints "may perform differently". The matched evaluation of these four heads that is being run separately is the only thing that can settle the ranking, and this report does not anticipate its outcome.

What the cards do support is a judgment about training provenance. WhiFlash and Tengyunw both document on-policy training data, meaning responses regenerated by Qwen3-8B, at the scale the recipe calls for; WhiFlash also documents a schedule that follows the EAGLE-3 paper closely. RedHatAI's card does not state the data amount or whether responses were regenerated, and Red Hat describes that release as coming from preliminary training code ([Red Hat blog](https://developers.redhat.com/articles/2025/11/19/speculators-standardized-production-ready-speculative-decoding)). AngelSlim's card states nothing about training. On documentation alone, WhiFlash and Tengyunw are the better-supported candidates, but that is a prior and not a measurement.

The one card number that speaks directly to next-token prediction is RedHatAI's acceptance length at k=1, which is 1.62-1.73. Subtracting the token the base model always contributes, the head's first drafted token is accepted about 62-73% of the time at temperature 0.6 with top-p and top-k sampling; this subtraction is an inference from the card's table and not a figure the card states.

The file sizes differ for a packaging reason. WhiFlash reports 0.4B parameters and RedHatAI's file holds 1,022,037,632 ([HF API record](https://huggingface.co/api/models/RedHatAI/Qwen3-8B-speculator.eagle3)). The likely explanation is that the RedHatAI file also stores a copy of the frozen 151,936 by 4096 embedding table, about 0.62B parameters; no card states this, so treat it as an inference. Other public Qwen3-8B heads exist but are poor fits: a multilingual research preview trained on only about 2,100 conversations, whose English acceptance length is 1.47 ([SwitchXDDD card](https://huggingface.co/SwitchXDDD/multilingual-eagle3-qwen3-8b)), and a Chinese-language variant listed in the EAGLE README.

## EAGLE-3 heads see three layers and speak a 32K vocabulary

Four properties of these heads shape what an interpretability study can conclude from them.

First, **the head does not read only the last hidden state.** EAGLE-3 concatenates low-, mid- and high-layer trunk features and reduces them with a linear layer of shape 3 x hidden to hidden before the decoder layer ([EAGLE-3 training code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/SafeAILab/EAGLE/main/eagle/traineagle3/cnets.py)). A finding about "what the head can predict" is therefore a finding about information spread across three depths of the trunk. The exact layer indices were not extracted in the notes and should be read from each checkpoint's config.

Second, the training target is the base model's own behaviour and not ground-truth text. The official code minimises a soft cross-entropy between the frozen target's next-token distribution and the head's output, and the speculators implementation uses KL divergence to the target's logits ([speculators Eagle-3 page](https://docs.vllm.ai/projects/speculators/en/latest/user_guide/algorithms/eagle3/)). This is the right objective for the reader's purpose, since the head is a learned model of what Qwen3-8B will say.

Third, the head has its own output layer over a **reduced draft vocabulary of 32,000 tokens**, chosen by token frequency in the training data, against Qwen3's 151,936. In training, positions where the target's top token falls outside the draft vocabulary are masked out of the loss, and at inference such tokens can never be proposed ([WhiFlash card](https://huggingface.co/WhiFlash/Qwen3-8B-EAGLE3)). The head's predictions are therefore undefined for rare tokens, and coverage degrades sharply outside English: an English-trained head scores acceptance length 2.37 on English, 1.36 on Hindi and 1.07 on Gujarati ([SwitchXDDD card](https://huggingface.co/SwitchXDDD/multilingual-eagle3-qwen3-8b)). Each head's 32K set depends on its own training data, so the four heads do not necessarily share a vocabulary, and any matched comparison has to account for that.

Fourth, thinking mode. RedHatAI's card states the head "was trained with thinking turned disabled", AngelSpec's Qwen3-8B benchmarks run with thinking off, and no published Qwen3-8B head claims training on thinking traces; the other three cards do not state the setting. No source measures acceptance inside thinking spans for Qwen3. The only precedent is that the EAGLE-3 authors added a reasoning dataset when targeting a reasoning model. Expect the published heads to be weaker predictors of Qwen3-8B's reasoning text than of its final answers, and treat that as untested.

If the research design needs a head that reads only the final hidden state, the EAGLE-3 heads do not qualify, and the relevant work is the MTP-style retrofit. AngelSpec trains the DeepSeek/Qwen block (normalised hidden state and token embedding, linear fusion, one transformer layer, shared embedding and output head) on frozen Qwen3-8B and reports a **mean acceptance length of 3.24** at temperature 1 with thinking off, against 4.57-5.41 for five-layer block drafters on the same data ([AngelSpec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25852)). That head has the attractive property of predicting over the full vocabulary through the base model's own output layer. The paper does not give its parameter count, dataset size, GPU-hours or per-position acceptance, does not compare it with EAGLE-3, and the notes could not confirm that a Qwen3-8B MTP checkpoint is downloadable. GrowMTP grows the same block from random initialisation on Qwen3-4B and reaches acceptance length 2.91 on in-domain math and 2.1-2.3 on held-out benchmarks; an offline-trained equivalent cost 161.6 GPU-hours on 8x H800, and no weights are linked ([GrowMTP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16648)). The speculators MTP trainer does not help here, because it only fine-tunes a head the model already ships with ([speculators MTP docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/projects/speculators/en/latest/user_guide/algorithms/mtp/)). AngelSpec's framework includes a Qwen3-8B single-node quickstart on 4 GPUs, which is the shortest path to training such a head ([AngelSlim](https://github.com/Tencent/AngelSlim)).

## Conclusion

The question "how hard is the retrofit" has shifted from a research problem to a selection problem. For Qwen3-8B the work is done four times over, and the open question is which of the four heads best models the base, which only a matched evaluation can answer. That evaluation should score what the reader cares about, namely agreement with Qwen3-8B's own next-token distribution at each draft depth, on shared prompts, at fixed sampling settings, with separate accounting for tokens outside each head's 32K vocabulary and for thinking-mode text. Acceptance length under a serving configuration is a proxy for that and a noisy one.

The deeper implication for interpretability is that the choice between an EAGLE-3 head and an MTP-style block is a choice of what the head is evidence about. An EAGLE-3 head shows what three trunk layers jointly make predictable; a last-layer block shows what survives into the final hidden state, which the literature suggests is less, because a trunk trained only for next-token prediction has no pressure to keep future-token information there. If the matched evaluation shows the published heads are good enough, reuse one and document its layer inputs and vocabulary. If the research question turns on the final hidden state alone, the evidence says a frozen-trunk MTP block is trainable for Qwen3-8B at a cost of tens to low hundreds of GPU-hours, and nobody has yet published the comparison between the two head types on this model.
